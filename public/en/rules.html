Tactic Master rules

Tactic Master plays like checkers and is won like a football match: move one piece one square, push the ball in a straight line, score in the opposing goal. No dice, no cards, no reflexes. This page is the complete reference — how a turn works, the edge cases, the three rule tiers and the five powers.

The board

The pitch is 7 columns by 9 rows, 63 squares in total. It is deliberately narrower than a checkerboard: with six pieces a side, a bigger board would produce slack games where nobody ever meets.

Each side defends a three-square goal — the three central squares of its back row. Blue occupies the bottom of the pitch and attacks upwards; red does the opposite. The two halves mirror each other exactly, so neither side starts with a positional advantage.

The ball starts at the centre of the board, and blue kicks off.

The pieces

Each team fields six pieces: one goalkeeper, two defenders and three forwards. Those names describe starting positions, not abilities — a defender and a forward move in exactly the same way, and either can score. Only the goalkeeper has rules of its own.

The tactical formation adds one defender and one forward per side, for eight against eight. The pitch gets denser and passing lanes become much harder to open. It is the default formation of the Expert tier.

The goalkeeper

The goalkeeper follows two special rules, and nothing else:

  • It only moves along the three squares of its own goal line, sliding sideways. It never advances, and never leaves the back row.
  • It covers no square at all (see defensive cover below). It defends purely by physically standing on one square of the goal.

That second rule is deliberate. If the keeper projected a cover zone like every other piece, the goal would be mathematically impossible to breach and the game would have no point. A keeper plugs one hole: the one it is standing on.

How a turn works

Blue moves first, then the sides alternate. On your turn you take one action, and only one:

  1. Move a piece. Your turn ends there — unless that piece lands next to the ball, in which case you may follow up with a pass (still optional).
  2. Push the ball with a piece already touching it. Select the piece, click a square along the path. No prior move is needed.

So a turn is worth either a move, or a pass, or a move followed by a pass when the moved piece ends up beside the ball. You can never pass twice in the same turn.

Moving

A piece moves one square in any of the eight directions, diagonals included. The destination square must be empty — of friendly pieces, of enemy pieces, and of the ball. You never step onto the ball, never capture it, never carry it. You step beside it in order to push it.

There are no captures in Tactic Master. No piece ever leaves the pitch. All sixteen (or twenty) pieces are still there at the final whistle.

Passing

A piece adjacent to the ball — in any of the eight directions, diagonals included — can push it. The ball travels in a straight line in the chosen direction and slides until it meets the first obstacle: a piece of either colour, or the edge of the pitch.

The point that most new players miss: every free square along the path is a valid destination. The ball does not have to travel all the way. You decide where it stops, and a one-square push is a perfectly legal move — often the best one.

The ball never passes through a piece. That is why a well-placed opposing piece is worth a wall: it does not need to intercept anything, it just needs to be on the line.

Defensive cover

This is the mechanic that turns the game into a tactical one, and the only rule that takes a little thought.

A square is covered by a team if an outfield piece of that team stands orthogonally adjacent to it — directly above, below, left or right. An opposing pass can neither stop on a covered square nor travel through it.

Three details decide a great many games:

  • Cover is strictly orthogonal. The four diagonal squares around a piece are never covered. A defence that looks airtight head-on is almost always porous at an angle.
  • The goalkeeper covers nothing, as noted above.
  • Only your opponent's cover blocks your passes. Your own pieces never obstruct you by cover — only by physically sitting on the path.

The two special squares

Crossing from a wing

When the ball sits on an edge column (the first or the seventh), the pass leaving it ignores enemy cover entirely. This is the game's cross: the direct counter to a defence packed into the middle.

The penalty spot

The penalty spot is the central square two rows from the opposing goal. A shot fired from there towards the goal gets two effects: it ignores cover, and it pierces one outfield defender — one only, and never the goalkeeper, nor one of your own pieces.

Getting the ball there usually costs two or three turns of build-up. That is exactly the intent: the game rewards preparation, not luck.

The one-two

After a pass, if the ball comes to rest orthogonally adjacent to one of your own outfield pieces, your team immediately plays again — but a move only, never a second pass.

The bonus does not stack: a one-two cannot trigger another one, and it does not combine with the Relay power. It is a tempo gained, not a chain reaction.

Scoring

A goal is scored the moment the ball comes to rest on any of the three squares of the opposing goal. Nothing else is required — no minimum distance, no number of passes, no piece in the box.

After a goal, everything resets as at kick-off: ball at the centre, all pieces back to their starting squares. The team that conceded restarts play. The first team to three goals wins the match.

Draws and shoot-outs

In time-limited formats the game stops after a fixed number of turns. If the scores are level, the match is a draw and is settled by a penalty shoot-out — a separate arcade mode with aiming and a power gauge. In a free game there is no turn limit: you play to the third goal.

The stall rule

Two cautious players could in theory shuffle pieces forever. The engine steps in: after eight consecutive turns without a single pass — four per side — the ball is returned to the centre for a neutral restart, provided the centre square is free. The pieces are not moved. The threshold is deliberately high; it never fires in a game that is actually progressing.

Classic is the default. In Discovery no pass is ever intercepted — the ball only stops against a piece or an edge, which makes the game instantly readable for a child or a first try. In Expert, all four mechanics are live on an eight-piece board. Advanced options let you switch each mechanic on or off individually.

The powers

When powered players are enabled, one randomly chosen outfield piece per team receives an ability, usable once per match. After that it becomes an ordinary piece again.

What Tactic Master does not have

  • No captures: no piece ever leaves the pitch.
  • No offside, no fouls, no cards, no corners, no throw-ins.
  • No randomness: no dice, no cards, no draws. Both players see everything, always — perfect information, as in chess.
  • No clock: the game is turn-based, think as long as you like.
  • No differentiated piece movement: apart from the keeper, every piece moves identically.
